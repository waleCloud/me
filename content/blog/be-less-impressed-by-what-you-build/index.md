For most of my career and generally for any software engineer pre-LLM-coding-agents era, being able to build things felt like a superpower.

Give me a problem, enough time, and access to the right tools, and I could usually figure out a way to make something work.

That ability has served me well as an engineer. It has opened doors, helped me lead teams, build products, start companies and turn ideas that existed only in conversations into software people could actually use.

For a long time, I also thought it gave a significant advantage as a founder.

I'm beginning to think there is a trap in that.

**The best technical founders may need to become less impressed by what they can build.**

> Engineers are trained to solve problems.
>
> We see friction and immediately start decomposing it.
>
> What system is responsible?
>
> What API do we need?
>
> Could this be automated?
>
> How should the data model work?
>
> What happens at scale?
>
> There is satisfaction in taking something messy and turning it into something elegant, refined and structured.
>
> But the business of market solutions doesn't particularly care about elegance.

A technically interesting problem may not necessarily be an important problem.

A difficult problem is not necessarily a valuable problem.

And a product working exactly as intended is not proof that enough people care about it or want it.

I knew all of this intellectually but building has forced me to understand it differently.

When someone tells an engineer that something is inconvenient, **we can hear a product requirement.**

When a prospect tells a founder that something is inconvenient, I've learned that the more useful response might be:

**So what?**

How painful is it?

How often does it happen?

What are you doing about it today?

What does doing nothing cost you?

Who inside the company cares enough to fight for this to change?

Will they pay to make it go away?

Those questions are increasingly more interesting to me now than whether I know how to build the solution.

### "That's impressive" is not the same as "I need this"

This is another thing I think technical founders have to unlearn.

**Compliments.**

You spend days or weeks building something complicated. You show it to someone and hear:

"That's really cool."

"That's impressive."

"I can see how companies would use this."

It feels good.

Especially when you know how much work sits behind what looks like a simple demo.

But I've become much more interested in what happens after the compliment.

Does the person immediately start describing where this would fit into their company?

Do they introduce someone else who needs to see it?

Do they ask how quickly they can start?

Do they ask about security or integration because they're already imagining deployment?

Do they try to find budget?

Do they actually come back and use it?

Or do they tell you you've built something impressive and carry on with their day?

Those are very different signals.

> One of the more dangerous things about being a technical founder is that ... **the product can keep giving you dopamine even when the market isn't giving you validation.**

There is always another feature.

Another integration.

Another workflow.

Another architecture improvement.

Another model to experiment with.

Another clever way of solving the problem.

And because you're building, it feels like you're progressing.

Sometimes you are.

Sometimes you're just extremely busy avoiding a much more uncomfortable question:

**Does anyone care enough?**

That is the question I'm learning to sit with for longer.

The engineer in me still loves building.

I don't think that will change.

But I'm trying to become less impressed by the fact that I can build something.

The more interesting questions now are:

Should this exist?

Who urgently needs it?

What changes for them if it works?

And what evidence do I have beyond the fact that I managed to make it work?

Maybe technical ability is most useful when it gives you the freedom to explore a problem quickly.

But it becomes dangerous when it gives you endless reasons to avoid admitting that the problem might not matter enough.

So these days, when I build something and my first reaction is:

**"This is pretty impressive."**

I'm trying to follow it with a harder question:

**"Yes. But does anybody actually need it?"**
